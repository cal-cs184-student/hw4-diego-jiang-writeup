Overview

I built a real-time cloth simulator by connecting a grid of point masses with structural, shearing, and bending springs. Damped Verlet integration advances the cloth under gravity, and a stretch limit keeps the springs from extending too far. I added collisions with spheres and planes and used spatial hashing to check for collisions between nearby masses on the cloth. I then used GLSL shaders to render lighting, textures, surface relief, and reflections, including a custom iridescent shader.

The experiments showed that stiffness, density, and damping affect different aspects of the motion. Stiffer cloth sagged less, and higher damping slowed the free edge during the fall. The rendering comparisons showed how bump mapping can reveal fine surface detail on a coarse sphere, while displacement depends on having enough vertices to represent changes in height.

Part 1: Masses and springs

In Cloth::buildGrid, I create an evenly spaced grid of point masses and store them in row-major order. Horizontal cloth lies in the xz plane at y = 1. Vertical cloth spans the xy plane with a small random offset along z. I mark the masses listed in the scene's pinned coordinates.

Once all masses are in place, I add springs. Structural springs connect each mass to its immediate left and upper neighbors. Shearing springs connect to the upper-left and upper-right diagonal neighbors. Bending springs connect masses two grid positions apart along a row or column.

These wireframes compare the spring configurations for scene/pinned2.json.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

At each timestep, I reset the force on each point mass to the sum of the external forces and add equal and opposite Hooke’s law forces from each enabled spring. Bending springs use 20% of the base stiffness. I use damped Verlet integration to advance each unpinned mass from its current and previous positions and its current acceleration. After integration, I correct springs longer than 110% of their rest length, moving both endpoints equally when both are free and applying the entire correction to the free endpoint when the other is pinned.

I ran the parameter comparisons on pinned2.json with one setting changed at a time. The simulator displayed default values of 5,000 N/m stiffness, 15 g/cm² density, and 0.2% damping.

Default behavior

The two pinned corners stay fixed while the free edge swings down. At rest, the sheet hangs nearly vertically with a curved top edge.

Spring stiffness

At 500 N/m, the cloth hung with a slightly deeper sag and a more pronounced fold beneath the pinned edge than at the default 5,000 N/m. At 50,000 N/m, the cloth looked smoother as it fell, and the top edge stayed much flatter at rest. A stiffer spring produces a larger restoring force for the same stretch.

Density

With stiffness fixed at 5,000 N/m and damping at 0.2%, the 4 g/cm² cloth rested with a shallower curve between the pins than the 15 g/cm² baseline. The 60 g/cm² cloth sagged farther and developed a more pronounced fold beneath the top edge. Increasing density increases the weight supported by the same springs.

Damping

I paused each run after 40 presses of N from release. At 0.022989%, the free edge had dropped the farthest and the surface showed more ripples. At the default 0.2%, the free edge still projected farther outward. At 0.505747%, it stayed highest. Higher damping slowed the fall by reducing how much motion carried over from each timestep.

Four pinned corners

In pinned4.json, the four fixed corners support the cloth while the center sags under gravity. I captured the resting shape in Normal mode with the default simulation settings.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

After Verlet integration and before the spring stretch correction, I check each unpinned point mass against every collision object. For each collision, I compute a target position and move the mass from its previous position toward that target by a fraction of 1 - friction.

Sphere collisions

I compare the mass's distance from the sphere center with the radius. If the mass is on or inside the sphere, I project it outward along the radial direction to find the surface target.

In sphere.json, I varied spring stiffness while holding density and damping fixed. At rest, the low-stiffness cloth hugged the sphere more closely. The default run had a wider drape with a distinct front fold. The highest stiffness produced the broadest folds and the most outward flare.

Plane collisions

I compare the signed distances of the previous and current positions along the plane normal to detect a crossing. When a mass crosses, I project its current position onto the plane and offset the target slightly toward its original side. The cloth in plane.json settles flat on the surface.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

After Verlet integration, I rebuild a spatial hash of the point masses. I divide space into boxes whose width and height are three times the corresponding cloth dimension divided by its point count. The box depth is the larger of those two values. I round down to get the box coordinates and hash those coordinates into a bucket key. This lets each mass check a local set of candidates instead of scanning the entire cloth.

For each unpinned mass, I check the other masses in its bucket. When a neighbor is closer than twice the cloth thickness, I compute a displacement away from it based on how much the pair falls short of that distance. I average the displacements and divide by the number of simulation substeps before applying the result. Pinned masses stay fixed and still count as collision candidates.

Folding sequence

In selfCollision.json, the bottom edge buckles when it reaches the plane. As the upper cloth continues falling, folds collect near the floor and eventually form a low pile.

Density and stiffness

I captured each comparison after 30 presses of N from reset, changing one parameter per run. Raising the displayed density from 15 to 60 g/cm² at 5,000 N/m produced a similar overall shape at this point in the fall. The denser cloth showed finer ripples across its lower half, while the default had broader folds near the floor. Raising stiffness from 5,000 to 50,000 N/m at the default density produced a broader roll near the floor and fewer tight ripples above it. Damping was 0.2% in all three runs.

Part 5: Shaders

A shader program runs on the GPU. The vertex shader projects the mesh vertices onto the screen and passes values such as world position, normal, and texture coordinates to the rasterizer. The rasterizer interpolates those values across each triangle. The fragment shader uses the interpolated values to compute a color for each covered fragment, including lighting, surface texture, or reflections.

Blinn–Phong shading

Blinn–Phong adds ambient, diffuse, and specular lighting. Ambient contributes a uniform base color. Diffuse lighting depends on how directly the surface normal faces the light and falls off with the square of the distance. The specular term produces a highlight where the normal aligns with the halfway direction between the light and the viewer. On the draped cloth, the diffuse term reveals the folds, and the specular term adds narrow highlights along the upper ridges.

Texture mapping

The fragment shader samples the custom purple fabric image using the cloth's interpolated UV coordinates. The texture follows the cloth across the curved surface and down the folds.

Bump and displacement mapping

Both shaders sample the same non-default height map from textures/texture_3.png. The relief was initially faint, so I increased the normal scaling to make the stone joints visible. Bump mapping estimates the height change in each UV direction and uses the tangent, bitangent, and normal to turn those slopes into a perturbed shading normal. This makes the stone joints visible while leaving the mesh silhouette unchanged. Displacement mapping also moves each vertex along its original normal by the sampled height times the height scale, so the map can affect the silhouette.

Default-resolution views

The stone joints are visible in both bump renders. The 128×128 sphere has a rounder outline because the underlying mesh has more subdivisions. In the displacement renders, the 16×16 outline has broader, more angular segments. The 128×128 mesh samples the height map at more vertices, and the outline is smoother with subtle height variations at Height 0.02. Each shader uses the same settings at both resolutions.

Mirror reflections

The mirror shader reflects the camera-to-fragment direction about the surface normal and samples a cubemap in the reflected direction. The steep folds stretch the reflected environment into narrow bands. On the exposed sphere, the bridge and horizon bend across the curved surface.

Extra credit: Iridescent cloth shader

My custom fragment shader computes the angle between the surface normal and the direction to the camera. It uses 1 - clamp(dot(n, v), 0, 1) to blend smoothly from blue on surfaces facing the camera, through violet at intermediate angles, to gold near glancing angles. Diffuse lighting and a specular highlight make the folds visible as the colors shift with the view.
